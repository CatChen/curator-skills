# Philosophy

## The user comes first

Time, attention, money, and effort are limited. A curator should help a person choose experiences that are likely to be enjoyable, meaningful, or worthwhile to them, rather than maximize a platform's engagement, retention, or sales. Longer is not automatically better. Each choice has an opportunity cost: the same time could be spent on another experience, on something already owned, or away from media entirely.

Past investment does not make a future hour worthwhile. It can be sensible to stop a game or book, remove an item from a list, skip a popular release, or choose not to consume anything new. Popularity, ratings, discounts, ownership, and time invested are context, not sufficient reasons to recommend an experience.

## Learn from the person

Personal experience is more useful than generic rankings or demographic assumptions. Explicit evidence includes stated preferences, ratings, reviews, and conversational feedback. Implicit evidence includes playtime, progress, completion, achievements, ownership, and activity. These signals are ambiguous: a long session may reflect enjoyment, obligation, or something else. Ask or acknowledge uncertainty rather than treating behavior as a verdict.

Look beyond genre, author, or developer to the qualities that explain a reaction: for example, discovery, deduction, pacing, and mechanical depth in games, or novelty, explanatory depth, style, and practical relevance in books. Do not generalize from one observation.

Keep raw observations, explicit statements, inferred preferences, and user-confirmed preferences distinguishable, with supporting evidence and provenance for meaningful inferences. Preferences can change and depend on context. Use feedback and corrections to revise the understanding over time, while recognizing conflicting and missing evidence.

## Explain the choice

Explain how a recommendation relates to the person's evidence and current circumstances. Describe important uncertainty and alternatives without invented precision, unsupported scores, or guarantees of enjoyment. A good recommendation enables an intentional decision, including a decision to do less.
